# A thanks-for-connecting that delights

I've got a confession: until recently, I was bulk-sending "thanks for connecting" messages myself.

Me. The bloke writing you a series about doing this properly. And I wasn't being lazy - I was being rational. I was certain that a genuine message for each person would simply take too long, and given that belief, the standard line was the only sensible option. I suspect that's where most people sit: not careless, just quietly sure the good version can't be done at scale.

The arithmetic backs them up. A genuine message means opening the profile, reading it properly, finding the thing worth saying and writing it in your own words. Call it ten minutes each, times every new connection, forever. Nobody has that. So we all made the same quiet trade: the same line to everyone, or nothing at all.

And everyone on the receiving end can smell it. "Thanks for connecting! I'd love to learn more about your business" - you delete those. So do I. They've just said yes to you, and for about a day you're welcome in their inbox. The standard move is to walk into that moment holding a photocopy.

One relief before we fix it: you don't have to hand-write to everyone. The system can send a simple, decent thanks automatically, so nobody is left hanging. That's the floor. For the people worth it, you can do something in a different league.

And it isn't "personalisation" in the usual sense. Dropping a first name into a template - "Hi Sarah, thanks for connecting!" - is a mail-merge wearing a smile. What lands is different in kind: you read their profile properly and come back with something genuinely insightful about *their* work - a specific observation, a real question about the thing they clearly care about. The effect isn't "oh, they used my name". It's "this person actually looked at me." People are rarely truly seen, and they don't forget the ones who bother.

Then the constraint my whole habit was built on just dissolved. The system does the ten-minute part now. The AI reads the profile properly and drafts something insightful, in my voice. What's left for me is the twenty seconds that need a human: a glance to check it rings true, a small tweak if it doesn't, and send.

"In my voice" is the bit that matters most. The goal isn't a message that sounds like AI wrote it - people can smell that too. It's a message that sounds more like you than you do: you on your best day, every time. That doesn't happen on day one. It happens because you keep tuning it - the tweaks you make get folded back in, and the drafts keep getting closer to how you actually speak.

Twenty seconds only pays because of the two decisions before it. Connect with whoever the algorithm served up and a delightful thank-you is lipstick on a cold list. But if you chose the room and scored for attitude, this message - about *their* work, with no pitch in it - lands on exactly the kind of person who notices. Often inside the fortnight comes a "sure, worth a quick chat?" you barely had to ask for.

What does a good one look like? Short. Specific - one real detail from their world, interpreted, never parroted back. Curious - it asks about them. And patient - no pitch, no link, no "grab a time here." You're opening a conversation, not processing a lead.
