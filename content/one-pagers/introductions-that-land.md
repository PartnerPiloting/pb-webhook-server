# Most introductions die in the inbox

You've had one of these. An email lands: *"Jane, meet Tom. Tom, meet Jane. You two should definitely connect!"* You both reply-all - "great to meet you, happy to chat!" - and then nothing happens. A week later neither of you can remember why you were meant to talk.

It's nobody's fault, exactly. Whoever sent it meant well. But they handed two busy people a puzzle - *why am I talking to this person, and who goes first?* - and busy people don't solve puzzles. They file them.

We tend to think of an introduction as a courtesy - a name passed along, a nice gesture. It's more than that. **An introduction is a small piece of writing whose whole job is to make the first conversation obvious.** When it does that job, two people talk. When it doesn't, you've spent a favour and got nothing for anyone.

Four things do the job.

**It's two-sided.** Each person learns who the other is and why they matter. Not "meet my friend Tom" - a few lines on each, so both of them arrive already interested.

**Facts, not adjectives.** "Tom's amazing, you'll love him" tells Jane nothing she can use. "Tom's a fractional CFO for growing trade businesses, and he's put several exceptional people my way" tells her exactly who he is. Facts are also what she can repeat to someone else - which is how an introduction keeps travelling after it lands.

**A reason to talk.** One or two lines on why these two, and why now. It doesn't have to be a business fit. Some of the best introductions I make are simply two good people who'll be brilliant at recommending each other, once each knows what the other offers. Say that, plainly, and the reason is there.

**One person makes the first move.** Ask one of them - usually the one who'll get the most from it - to reach out, and put the other person's email address right there in the body. Two people who are both "happy to chat" will each politely wait for the other. One person with a clear next step and an address in front of them will just send the email.

And one habit before any of it: **say it out loud first.** When you catch yourself thinking "they should meet so-and-so" on a call, say it. *"I know someone you should meet - mind if I introduce you?"* You get their yes, they see you were really listening, and the email that follows is expected rather than out of the blue.

Get those right and something shifts. Your introductions stop being pleasantries and start being the thing people remember you for. The replies come back as "we've met - thank you, that was gold", not "sounds great". And each one quietly makes you more worth knowing.

Which leaves one practical question. Writing like this takes a few careful minutes each time - fine for one introduction, but a lot to ask when you're making several a week on top of everything else. That's the last piece.
