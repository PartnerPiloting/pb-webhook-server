# Every link is homework

You've just had a good first call. You want them to understand what you do, so you do what everyone does: you send the lot. The article, the explainer video, the deck, the landing page, maybe that TED talk that says it better than you can. Five links, all good, all relevant.

And they read none of them.

That isn't because they aren't interested. You've handed a busy person an assignment. Five links is a reading list, and a reading list gets filed under "later", which really means never. Worse, you've spent the good stuff in one go. The piece that would have landed perfectly in three weeks' time got skimmed, or skipped, on a Tuesday afternoon when they weren't ready for it.

The old habit comes from a reasonable place. We think of our material as proof - the more of it they see, the more convinced they'll be. So we send everything, just in case one of them hits.

But persuasion doesn't work in bulk. **It works in sequence.** People come round to a new idea a step at a time, and each piece of your material is built for a particular step. A piece that explains the problem only works before they've heard your answer. A piece about the numbers only works once they're already wondering whether it's worth it - sent earlier, it ends their thinking instead of starting it.

So here's the shift. Stop thinking of your links as a pile of proof and start thinking of them as a library, where every piece has a note on the spine saying when it earns its place. Three questions cover almost everything:

- **When does it go?** After the first call. The morning of the second. Only once I've explained the idea out loud.
- **Who is it for?** People frustrated that what they're doing isn't working. Anyone heading down the consulting path.
- **Who must never get it?** Not for financial services - the angle lands wrong there. Never for founders who've already made the leap.

Here's how mine run. After a first call, two links and no more: an article that picks up where our conversation left off, then the one page that sets out the whole idea. Two that land beat four that get skimmed. My two-part deck goes problem first, then the answer only after I've walked them through it on a call - never both in the same email. My pricing page waits until the second or third conversation, or until someone asks.

Look at what that does. Each email now carries one piece that fits exactly where that person is. It reads as care, not a brochure - *he remembered what I said, and he sent the one thing that answers it.* And because you haven't used everything up in the first week, you always have something worth sending next. Your library becomes a supply of reasons to follow up, handed out one at a time.

You don't need a big library to start. You need one good "here's what I do" link and the discipline to write the note beside it. Add the rest as you notice yourself pasting the same link twice. The note is the valuable part, and once it's written, the tools can hold it for you and pick the right piece each time. You stop deciding afresh in every email.

So have a look at what you sent after your last good call. How many links were there? And which one did they actually open?
