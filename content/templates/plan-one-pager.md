# The plan one-pager

For someone who has said yes and now has to wait for session one. The gap between the yes and the
first session is where enthusiasm leaks, and it is also where a client turns up with no idea what
is about to happen and no homework done. This page fills both holes.

Written for quick deciders first, but it suits everyone. The non-technical client gets told that
almost nothing is being asked of them. The technical client gets the architecture and the reason
for each piece without needing a call to ask.

**When it goes:** one or two days before session one. Not the same day as the yes - that slot
belongs to the "Let's get you started" email, which carries the join link and the numbers. This is
the second beat, not the first.

**What it does:** names every session, every step inside it, and what each step enables - in the
client's own language, taken from the call. Boring on purpose. The reassurance is the point.

**What it never does:** re-sell, re-quote the fee, list the tools as a shopping list, or hedge.
They already decided. Treat the decision as settled and talk about the work.

**Length:** it will run to a page and a half and that is fine. The heading "The whole process,
spread over several sessions" licenses the length instead of apologising for it. Do not cut the
"why" lines to save space - they are the whole product.

**Voice:** Guy, first person. Australian English, plain spaced short dash, never an em dash. Claims
about how good the system is stay modest and specific: name a thing the client watched happen on
the call rather than reaching for an adjective. Under-claim rather than over-claim - "the machinery
that can do a hell of a lot of it" beats "the machinery that does it", because the client does it
and the tool helps.

---

## The shape

Six blocks, in this order.

### 1. The opener - two or three flat sentences

One plain fact about their business, stated without admiration, then the promise of the page.

> You've got advisors' calendars to fill. This is the machinery that can do a hell of a lot of it.
> Here's the order we build it in.

**Written fresh every time, from the transcript.** The fact must be something they said about their
own job, not a story about them. Reciting someone's own week back to them with admiration built in
reads as patronising, however well meant. Facts, then a short dry verdict - the same register as
the "Let's get you started" email.

### 2. The length note

> **The whole process, spread over several sessions**
>
> It's a long read. That's deliberate - I'd rather you knew why we're doing each piece than turn up
> and have things done to you.

### 3. Session one - the plumbing

Standing content. Four steps, each with a bolded plain-English heading written as an action, one
short paragraph of what actually happens, then bullets of what it enables.

The order inside the session matters: connector, calendar and mailbox, then the proof, and the
Anthropic key LAST. The key is card details and a fiddly console. If it overruns it should overrun
after the client has already watched Wingguy read their real diary, not before.

### 4. After session one - homework, and the machine

Two jobs, both in their own time, both framed as things that get reviewed rather than tested.

The machine gets the most words on the page, because it is the largest invisible thing done for
them and because an unexplained $20 a month reads as an upsell. It needs all four parts:

- **Why it cannot live on the laptop** - Linked Helper only works while it is running, and a laptop
  sleeps, restarts and takes updates without telling anyone. Five days of nothing, discovered late.
- **What it is instead** - a small Linux machine in an Australian data centre, dedicated to this one
  job. No Windows, no sleep, no uninvited restarts. Cheap and bulletproof. Their account, their
  card, cancel any time, nothing tangled up in Guy's.
- **Their ten minutes** - they open their own Claude and say "Help me set up my Linked Helper
  machine". This doubles as the first real demonstration that the connector works, which is why it
  is worth more than an instruction sheet. Passwords by text or Zoom chat, never email.
- **Guy's build** - desktop, Linked Helper, watchdog, nightly backup, five-minute monitoring. Say
  the 2am line: something Guy sees and fixes, not something the client discovers a week later.

Send the machine ask the same day as session one, so there are days of slack before the sign-in.

### 5. Sessions two and three

Session two is **a review, not a test** - say so in those words. The commonest reason a call gets
pushed is "I haven't done the homework yet", and a call that proceeds either way never gives that
excuse a chance to form. It also carries the first LinkedIn draft in their voice, which is the
moment worth having with someone rather than leaving them to it alone.

Session three is the coaching one: who they are actually going after, and what the first message
says. Keep the Linked Helper sign-in (mechanical, five minutes) away from it - bolting the two
together is what made Rick Wong's build run two and a half hours.

Then a short background block: meeting link, extension, dress rehearsal, recorder. Least
load-bearing thing on the page, and the first to cut if it has to come down to one page.

### 6. Where this lands, and the one thing before

**Written fresh every time, from the transcript.** One short paragraph entirely inside their
business - their words, their customers, their problem. Then exactly one thing to do before session
one, with the link. Put it last: they get sold on the plan first, then given the small job.

## Where the facts come from

The session content is **not** owned by this file. It is `docs/concierge-run-sheet.md` and
`docs/wingguy-onboarding-checklist.md`. Read those before writing the page, and check the session
lengths against what is actually booked in the diary - a 30-minute slot does not hold an hour's run
sheet, and promising it is worse than not sending the page at all.
